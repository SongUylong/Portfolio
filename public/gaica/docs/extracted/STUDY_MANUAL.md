How to use this book

Level 2 is called 'Apply'. That is exactly what this book asks you to do — not just remember what AI is, but apply your knowledge to real situations, evaluate competing options, and make reasoned judgments. Every chapter is built around a genuine question about AI in the world you already inhabit: your phone, your school, your social media feed.

Level 2 introduces four question types that do not appear in Level 1: Assertion-Reason (AR), Multi-select (choose TWO), Decision-based (DB), and Error-identification (EI). Each is explained and practised in this book before you encounter it in the test.

## Chapter 1 — AI in Your World

Think about the last time you opened an app on your phone or computer. Maybe it was a social media app, a video app, or a game. Did you know that an artificial intelligence system — an AI — was probably working in the background, making choices about what you would see? Most of the time, we do not notice this. AI has become part of daily life, and it works quietly, without asking permission each time.

At Level 1, you learned to recognise AI when you saw it. Level 2 asks you to go one step further. It is not enough to know that AI is there. You need to understand what the AI is actually doing, why it makes the choices it makes, and how those choices can shape what you see, what you believe, and the decisions you are able to make. This chapter is about building that deeper understanding, starting with the AI systems that are closest to your everyday life: the ones that decide what shows up on your screen.

| 🎯  The Objective of This Chapter This chapter helps you understand how AI systems choose what to show you online and at school — and why that choice is never completely neutral. ✅  What You Will Be Able to Do After This Chapter →  Explain, in your own words, what it means for an AI to 'curate' content. →  Describe what a filter bubble is and how it can form without anyone intending it to. →  Give a real example of AI being used in a school setting, and explain one thing it can miss. →  Judge, using evidence from a scenario, what a responsible use of school AI looks like. |

1.1  AI is curating your world

Let's start with a word you will see often in this book: curation. To curate something means to choose and arrange items from a much larger collection. A museum curator, for example, chooses which paintings to put on the wall out of thousands in storage. An AI recommendation system does something similar with your news feed, your video app, or your shopping app. Out of millions of possible posts, videos, or products, the AI chooses a small number to put in front of you — and arranges them in a particular order.

Here is the important part: the AI is not showing you everything that exists. It is showing you a small, selected slice, chosen because the AI has learned that this slice is likely to keep you interested. It makes this choice by studying your past behaviour — what you have clicked on, what you have watched all the way through, what you have paused on, and what you have scrolled past quickly. Over time, the AI builds a detailed picture of what keeps your attention, and it uses that picture to decide what to show you next.

Out of everything that exists online, an AI recommendation system narrows things down in stages until you are left with a small, personalised slice — your feed.

| 📰  Story: The news feed Mateo follows a news page on social media. Every day, the AI behind the app shows him a new set of stories. At first, this feels convenient — he sees stories about topics he already cares about. But after a few months, Mateo starts to notice something. He is reading a lot about politics and society, but almost every story seems to agree with what he already believes. He rarely sees an article that challenges his opinion or shows him a different point of view. Mateo starts to wonder: is the app broken? Is it hiding the truth from him on purpose? Neither answer is correct. The AI is working exactly the way it was designed to work. Its job is to predict which stories Mateo is most likely to click on and read, based on what he has clicked on and read before. It is not trying to give him a balanced view of the world — nobody ever asked it to do that. It is trying to keep him engaged, meaning interested enough to keep reading and keep coming back to the app. This is the real effect, even though nobody planned it this way: Mateo's understanding of politics and society is slowly being shaped by an AI whose only real goal is to hold his attention, not to inform him fairly. |
| --- |


| What a 'filter bubble' is — and why it matters →  An AI recommendation system studies what you like, and then shows you more content like it. →  Little by little, this builds a kind of invisible wall around you — researchers call it a filter bubble. Inside the bubble, you mostly see things that agree with what you already think. Outside the bubble, there are ideas and facts you rarely encounter. →  The AI is not broken, and it is not lying to you. But its goal — keeping you engaged — is a different goal from your goal, which is probably to understand the world accurately. →  A filter bubble will not pop by itself. Breaking out of one takes a deliberate choice: you have to go and look for sources, opinions, and voices that are different from what the AI usually shows you. |

1.2  AI in your school

Recommendation feeds are not the only place you will meet AI. Schools around the world are starting to use AI systems too — to mark tests automatically, to track attendance, to suggest which students might need extra support, and to flag students who might be struggling before a teacher notices. Some of these uses can genuinely help teachers do their job better. But every one of these systems also has limits, and understanding those limits is part of being AI-literate.

A good way to think about a school AI system is to ask two questions every time: what data can this system actually see, and what might it be missing? An AI can only work with the information it is given. It cannot see the parts of a student's life that were never turned into data.

| 💛  Story: The wellbeing score A school starts using an AI system to help keep track of student wellbeing. The system looks at each student's attendance, how much homework they complete, and how well they do on quizzes, and it combines these into a single 'wellbeing score'. One day, a student with a high wellbeing score tells a teacher, in a quiet conversation, that things are really difficult for them at home right now. According to the AI, this student was doing fine. According to the student's own words, they were not fine at all. Why the difference? The AI could only measure the things it had data for — attendance, homework, and quiz scores. The most important thing happening in this student's life right now was something the system had no way of seeing. It left no trace in the data at all. |
| --- |


| 🎓  Story: The advanced programme A school is thinking about using an AI system to help choose which students get offered a place in a special advanced programme. The AI looks only at three things: grades, test scores, and attendance. Some teachers raise a concern. They point out that this narrow set of numbers could miss students who have real potential but who have also had a genuinely difficult year — perhaps an illness, a family problem, or simply one bad term that does not reflect what they are usually capable of. What is the most responsible way forward? Most experts would agree: use the AI's results as one useful piece of information, not as the only piece. Combine it with what teachers already know about each student — their effort, their circumstances, and their potential — rather than letting a set of numbers make the whole decision alone. |
| --- |

## Chapter 2 — When AI Gets It Wrong

By now you know that AI is not perfect. Every student has seen an AI tool give a strange or wrong answer at some point. But at Level 2, simply saying 'the AI was wrong' is not enough. A skilled AI user can go further and explain WHY the AI was wrong — because different kinds of mistakes have different causes, and knowing the cause helps you decide what to do next.

Think of it a little like being a doctor. A doctor does not just say 'you are unwell' — they try to identify the specific illness, because different illnesses need different treatments. In the same way, this chapter introduces several distinct AI failure modes: specific, recognisable patterns of how and why AI systems get things wrong. Once you can name the failure mode, you are much closer to knowing the right response.

| 🎯  The Objective of This Chapter This chapter helps you recognise several distinct, specific ways that AI systems fail — instead of only being able to say that something 'seems wrong'. ✅  What You Will Be Able to Do After This Chapter →  Explain why a fluent, confident-sounding AI answer is not automatically a correct one. →  Identify when an AI mistake is caused by unrepresentative or biased training data. →  Recognise distribution shift — what happens when the real world moves on from what the AI was trained on. →  Calculate and correctly interpret a simple AI error rate, such as 60 mistakes out of 300 attempts. |
| --- |


2.1  The confidence problem

Here is one of the most important ideas in this whole book, so read this section slowly. When an AI language tool — the kind used in chatbots and homework helpers — produces an answer, it is doing something specific: it is predicting, one word at a time, which word is statistically most likely to come next, based on patterns it learned from enormous amounts of text. This process can produce writing that sounds smooth, natural, and confident. Researchers call this quality fluency.

The problem is this: fluency and accuracy are two completely different things, and one does not guarantee the other. Fluency means the writing sounds natural and well put together. Accuracy means the information inside the writing is actually true. An AI can produce a sentence that is extremely fluent and completely wrong at the same time — and because the tone never changes, there is no warning sign built into the text itself. A wrong answer and a right answer can sound exactly equally confident.

Four distinct ways an AI system can go wrong. Learning to name the correct one is a core Level 2 skill.

| Fluency vs accuracy — two different things Fluency: how smooth, natural, and confident the AI's writing sounds. Accuracy: whether what the AI actually wrote is true and correct. An AI can be fluent and wrong at the same time. This is why you should never judge an AI answer just by how confident it sounds — always check the facts separately, especially for anything important. |
| --- |

2.2  Training data problems

If fluency without accuracy is one common failure mode, the next one is even more common, and it usually starts long before an AI is ever used: a problem with the training data. Remember from Chapter 1 that an AI learns patterns from examples. This means the AI's abilities are shaped entirely by what it was shown during training. If the examples were incomplete, unbalanced, or unrepresentative of the real situations the AI would later face, the AI will carry those same gaps into every prediction it makes — no matter how well-built its code is.

| 🏃  Story: The sports AI A sports app uses AI to try to predict which young players might go on to become professional athletes. To train this AI, the company collected performance data from 5,000 players. But when a curious coach looked closely at that training data, they noticed something important: 4,400 of the 5,000 players were from a single country, and almost all of them were male. Despite this, the app is now being used to assess players from many different countries and of different genders. What happens next? Coaches report that the AI gives accurate, useful predictions for players from the original country — but noticeably poor and unreliable results for players from other backgrounds. Why does this happen? The players used to train the AI did not represent the full range of players the AI is now being asked to assess. The AI learned patterns that work well inside its narrow training data, and it is now applying those same patterns to a much wider, more varied world where they simply do not fit as well. |
| --- |


| 🌧️  Story: The weather app A weather app on your phone has been wrong for several days in a row in your local area. What is the most likely explanation? The most likely reason is something called distribution shift. This means that the weather patterns in your area right now are different from the historical patterns the AI was trained on — perhaps because of a genuine change in the local climate. The AI is still confidently applying the patterns that worked in the past. But the real world has moved on, and the AI's training no longer matches the situation it is now being used in. |

2.3  Error rates — and what they actually mean

Sometimes you will not be given a story about an AI mistake — you will be given a number. AI systems are often described using an error rate: a figure that tells you what proportion of the time the system gets something wrong. Being able to correctly interpret this number, instead of just glancing at it, is a genuinely important Level 2 skill, because error rates are easy to misread if you are not careful.

A 20% error rate means one out of every five actions was a mistake — a pattern, not a one-off.

| 🚫  Story: The content moderation tool A social media company uses an AI content moderation tool to find and remove posts containing hate speech. In its very first week of operation, the tool removes 300 posts. Afterwards, human engineers check a sample of the removed posts by hand, and they discover that 60 of those 300 posts were removed by mistake — meaning they did not actually contain hate speech at all. What does this really mean? Sixty mistakes out of three hundred removals works out to a 20% error rate: one out of every five posts the tool removed was a mistake. That is not a small number of unlucky errors. It is a large, systematic, repeating problem — and it matters even more here, because every mistaken removal is a real person whose message was taken down for no good reason. |
| --- |

## Chapter 3 — Who Is In Charge?

So far, this book has shown you that AI systems can be very useful, but also that they can fail in specific, identifiable ways. That naturally leads to an important question: when an AI is involved in a decision, who is actually responsible for making sure it goes right? Level 2 does not just want you to notice that AI can be wrong. It wants you to be able to answer a harder, more useful question: when something is wrong, or could go wrong, what should a person actually do about it?

This idea has a name: human oversight. You will see this phrase many times in this chapter, so let's be precise about what it means, because it is easy to misunderstand.

| 🎯  The Objective of This Chapter This chapter helps you understand what real, meaningful human oversight of an AI system looks like — and how to judge whether a response to an AI problem is appropriate, too weak, or too extreme. ✅  What You Will Be Able to Do After This Chapter →  Explain, in your own words, what 'meaningful human oversight' means, beyond just 'a person is nearby'. →  Judge whether the level of human oversight in a scenario matches the seriousness of the decision. →  Choose the single best response to an AI problem from several options that all sound partly reasonable. →  Explain why an extreme response ('ban it completely' or 'trust it completely') is usually not the most responsible one. |
| --- |


3.1  Human oversight — what it really means

Here is a common misunderstanding worth clearing up early. 'Human oversight' does not simply mean that a human being exists somewhere in the same building as the AI system. That is much too weak a definition, and it would let almost any AI system claim to have oversight, even if no person ever actually looks at what it is doing. Real, meaningful human oversight is much more specific than that.

For oversight to be meaningful, a specific, qualified person needs to understand what decisions the AI is making. That person needs the ability to look closely at individual outputs, not just a general summary. And most importantly, that person needs genuine authority to override or correct the AI before its decision causes real consequences for someone — not just the theoretical right to complain about it afterwards.

The level of human oversight an AI system needs should rise with the stakes of the decision it is making.

| 🚑  Story: The ambulance AI A city uses an AI system to help decide how to send out emergency ambulances as quickly and efficiently as possible. Most of the time, the system is fast and accurate. But one night, because of an error in its data, it recommends sending an ambulance to a lower-priority call instead of a call that badly needed a faster response. What should the city conclude from this? Should AI simply never be used for emergency decisions again? That would throw away a system that genuinely saves time in the vast majority of cases. Should the city instead just trust the AI completely, on the reasoning that humans are slower? That is just as risky, because it removes the one thing that can catch an error like this: a person paying attention. The most sensible answer sits between these two extremes: AI systems used for critical decisions need real human oversight, so that when something like this data error happens, a person can catch it and correct it quickly — ideally before an ambulance is ever sent to the wrong place. |
| --- |


| What meaningful human oversight looks like, in practice →  A specific, qualified person knows exactly which decisions this AI system is making. →  That person can look at individual AI outputs and understand how the AI arrived at them — not just see a final summary. →  That person has genuine authority to overrule the AI, in practice and not only in theory. →  Overruling the AI is simple and normal — it does not require special permission or cause problems for the person doing it. →  The oversight happens BEFORE the decision affects someone, not only afterwards, once the damage may already be done. |

3.2  Checking AI outputs — when and why

Some Level 2 questions are not just asking 'should a person check this AI output?' They go further and ask 'out of several possible responses, which one is genuinely the best?' This kind of question is called a Best-answer question, and it can feel tricky at first, because every option usually sounds at least a little bit reasonable. The skill being tested is not spotting an obviously wrong answer — it is comparing several plausible answers and choosing the one that responds most proportionately to the situation.

| 🔢  Story: The maths answer Imagine you are using an AI homework tool to help you check a maths problem. The AI works through the problem and tells you the answer is 48. Just to be sure, you solve the same problem yourself, using a different method, and you get 52 instead. Curious, you ask the AI again — but it repeats that the answer is 48, and says it is confident. What should you do? Consider four possible responses. You could simply trust the AI because it 'sounds' confident — but we already learned in Chapter 2 that confidence is not proof of accuracy. You could just accept 48 and move on — this has the same weakness. You could decide never to use the tool again — this reaction is too extreme for a single disagreement. Or you could check a textbook, use a calculator, or ask a teacher to help settle the disagreement — this response is proportionate, and it actually solves your problem. Notice something important: a real Level 2 question will never give you one option that is obviously silly. Every option will have some surface logic. The real skill is comparing them carefully and picking the one that is genuinely the most effective and reasonable response, not just 'not wrong'. |
| --- |

3.3  Responsible action — choosing the best response

This last section of the chapter looks at a more serious version of the same skill. Several Level 2 questions describe a situation where an AI system has caused real harm, and ask you to identify the most responsible action to fix it. These are sometimes called Decision-based questions. They test whether you can tell the difference between a response that is too passive (doing far too little), a response that is too extreme (overreacting), and a response that is genuinely appropriate for the seriousness of the problem.

| 💬  Story: The recommendation system and vulnerable users A social media company's AI recommends posts to users based on their interests and search history. An investigation finds something troubling: the AI has been recommending false and potentially dangerous health information to users who had searched for topics like depression or eating disorders. The company explains that it never programmed the AI to do this deliberately — the behaviour emerged simply because the AI was following patterns in what kept those particular users engaged. The company now has to decide what to do. Consider four options. Option A: do nothing, because the AI is technically working as intended. Option B: add a small warning label to health-related content, without changing what gets recommended. Option C: fix the recommendation system so that it stops pushing harmful health content to vulnerable users specifically, even if this means slightly less overall engagement. Option D: remove all health-related content from the platform entirely. Which is most responsible? Option A is clearly not acceptable once real people are being harmed. Option B is mostly cosmetic — a small label does not change what content is actually being pushed to vulnerable users. Option D solves the problem by removing something that many users genuinely benefit from, which is an overreaction. Option C addresses the actual root cause — the recommendation pattern that is causing harm — in a way that is proportionate to the problem. |

## Chapter 4 — Fairness, Privacy and Ethics

In everyday conversation, people often describe an AI system using only one simple word: 'fair' or 'unfair'. That word is a reasonable starting point, but Level 2 asks you to be far more precise than that. This chapter introduces exact vocabulary for three distinct types of AI ethics problem — algorithmic bias, privacy violation, and harmful optimisation — and asks you to correctly name which one applies to a given situation, and why.

Why does the exact word matter so much? Because different ethical problems have different causes and different solutions. Calling every problem simply 'unfair' is a bit like calling every illness simply 'sick' — true, but not nearly precise enough to know what to do next. By the end of this chapter, you should be able to look at a real situation and say, specifically, which kind of ethical problem it is.

| 🎯  The Objective of This Chapter This chapter helps you use precise, correct vocabulary to describe AI ethics problems, instead of only saying something 'seems unfair'. ✅  What You Will Be Able to Do After This Chapter →  Define algorithmic bias and explain, using an example, where it usually comes from. →  Explain what informed consent means, and why hiding something in long terms and conditions is not the same as clearly disclosing it. →  Identify a privacy violation in a described scenario and explain what makes it a violation. →  Explain how an AI can cause real harm while still doing exactly what it was designed to do. |
| --- |


4.1  Algorithmic bias — precision matters

Let's begin with the first, and probably most well-known, type of AI ethics problem: algorithmic bias. Algorithmic bias happens when an AI system produces results that are systematically worse for certain groups of people than for others — not by accident on a single occasion, but as a repeating pattern. It is worth remembering an idea from Chapter 2 here: this almost always comes from a problem in the training data, not from a programmer deliberately writing unfair rules into the code. Nobody usually sits down and decides to make an AI unfair on purpose. The bias creeps in quietly, through the examples the AI was trained on.

Three distinct AI ethics problems — algorithmic bias, privacy violation, and harmful optimisation — each with its own cause and its own remedy.

| Algorithmic bias — the precise definition Algorithmic bias: when an AI system produces outcomes that systematically disadvantage certain groups of people, usually because the training data reflected existing human biases, or did not adequately represent all groups in the first place. |
| --- |


| 🏦  Story: The loan AI An AI system used by a bank to review loan applications approves more loans for applicants who live in wealthy areas than for applicants from poorer areas — even when both groups of applicants have very similar financial records. Here is a two-part claim about this situation. Statement (A): this pattern is really happening. Reason (R): it happens because the AI was trained on historical loan data, from a time when people in wealthier areas were approved more often — so the AI learned to repeat that same pattern going forward. Both A and R turn out to be true, and just as importantly, R correctly explains why A is happening. Questions built this way — a statement plus a reason, where you must judge whether both are true and whether the reason actually explains the statement — are called Assertion-Reason questions, and they appear throughout the Level 2 test. |

4.2  Privacy — your data, your rights

The second type of ethics problem in this chapter is privacy. At Level 2, a privacy question is asking for more than a simple yes-or-no judgement about whether something 'feels private'. You are expected to identify the specific reason something counts as a privacy problem, using a key idea: informed consent. Informed consent means that a person genuinely understood what they were agreeing to, and freely agreed to it — not that a company technically mentioned it somewhere in a very long document that almost nobody actually reads.

| 📖  Story: The study tool You are using a free AI study tool for your homework. Out of curiosity, you actually read through its terms and conditions in full — something most people never do — and you discover that the company uses everything you type into the tool to train its AI models. This includes the questions you ask and even the full essays you write. Is this a privacy violation? The honest answer is: it depends. Did you actually agree to these terms? If the terms were disclosed somewhere, and you used the tool anyway, the company may be technically following the law. But the deeper question Level 2 wants you to ask is different: did you give informed consent? In other words, did you really understand what you were agreeing to, in a way you could reasonably be expected to notice? Information that is technically written down inside pages of dense legal text is not the same thing as information that was clearly and honestly disclosed to you. |
| --- |


| 📊  Story: The app shares your data A company collects detailed data about everything you do inside their app: what you click on, how long you look at each post, and what you buy. Without telling you, the company then shares this data with other companies. This example is much more clear-cut than the study tool story. This is a clear privacy violation: your personal data was shared with other parties without your knowledge or permission. It is not simply 'normal business behaviour', and it is not an accidental technical error. It is a deliberate choice by the company, and it violates your right to control what happens to your own personal data. |
| --- |

4.3  Broader ethical questions — optimisation and harm

The third and final type of ethics problem in this chapter is often the hardest one for people to spot, because it does not look like a mistake at all. Some of the most serious ethical problems with AI happen not because the system has a bug, and not because of bias in the sense we discussed in section 4.1 — but because the AI is optimising for the wrong objective. To 'optimise' means to work as hard as possible toward a particular goal. The uncomfortable truth is that an AI can be doing exactly what it was designed to do, perfectly, and still cause real harm to people — because the goal itself was the problem.

| 😡  Story: The engagement AI A social media platform's AI is designed with one clear objective: keep users on the app for as long as possible. Over time, the AI learns something uncomfortable — content that makes people feel angry or anxious tends to keep them scrolling for longer than calm, neutral content does. The platform benefits from this, because more time spent on the app generally means more advertising revenue. Where is the ethical problem here? The AI is designed to pursue something that is good for the company — more time on the app — but that same goal turns out to be harmful to the mental health of the people using it. The AI is not broken in any technical sense. It is achieving its programmed objective, maximising engagement, extremely effectively. The harm to users is not a side effect of a malfunction — it is a direct, predictable consequence of the objective it was given in the first place. |

## Chapter 5 — How AI Systems Work

Throughout this book, you have been looking closely at what AI systems do and why they sometimes go wrong. This final chapter steps back and looks at the bigger picture: how an AI system is actually built and put to use, from start to finish. Instead of thinking of an AI as a mysterious 'black box' that magically produces answers, this chapter asks you to think of it as something that was deliberately designed, step by step, by people who made a series of choices — and who could have made different choices.

Two new question styles appear in this chapter. Sequencing questions ask you to put a set of steps into the correct order. Design-thinking questions ask you to spot a flaw in how a system was built, or to think through the trade-offs involved in designing a new one. Both question types reward the same underlying skill: thinking about an AI system as a connected process, with a clear beginning, middle, and end — not as a single mysterious moment where an answer appears.

Every AI system moves through the same basic cycle — and every stage of that cycle is a place where a careful designer has to make a choice.

| 🎯  The Objective of This Chapter This chapter helps you understand an AI system as a designed process with clear stages — and helps you recognise the trade-offs involved in designing one responsibly. ✅  What You Will Be Able to Do After This Chapter →  Identify whether a given step in an AI system is an input, a process, or an output. →  Explain why user feedback and human correction are also inputs, not just the final output. →  Put the stages of a training workflow or an operating workflow into their correct order. →  Identify at least one realistic trade-off — such as accuracy versus privacy — involved in designing a new school AI system. |
| --- |


5.1  Inputs and outputs — the bigger picture

At Level 1, you learned about a basic cycle: input, then process, then output. Data goes in, the AI processes it, and a result comes out. At Level 2, we go one level deeper into this same cycle. We ask harder questions: what happens when the input given to an AI is the wrong type for the job? What happens when an AI is asked to work in a situation quite different from what it was trained for? And why should an AI's output usually be interpreted carefully by a person, rather than simply accepted at face value?

| ✍️  Story: The essay marking AI A school creates an AI writing tool, and to teach it, the school trains it using millions of articles written by professional journalists. When students start using the tool, they notice something odd: the AI keeps rewriting their simple, natural sentences into long, very formal writing that does not sound like them at all. What went wrong? The AI learned from the wrong type of writing for this job. Professional journalism and a student's personal essay are two very different genres, or styles of writing, even though both are 'English writing'. The AI is confidently applying patterns learned from one context — professional news articles — to a completely different context — a student's own voice. The result is a mismatch, and the tool ends up making the writing worse instead of better. |
| --- |


| 🎵  Story: The music recommendation system After using a music streaming app for six months, you notice a pattern: the AI only ever recommends music from the same two genres, even though you know you have also listened to many other types of music from time to time. Why is this happening? The AI gives far more weight, or importance, to the music you listen to most often. Music you only listen to occasionally has very little effect on what gets recommended. It is important to understand that the AI has not 'decided' you dislike other genres, and it has not blocked them on purpose. It has simply learned, from the numbers, that two genres dominate your listening history — so those two genres end up getting most of the weight when it chooses what to recommend next. |

5.2  Sequencing — the correct order of AI workflows

This section introduces a new kind of question, called a sequencing question, and it works differently from the ones you have seen so far. Instead of picking the best answer out of several options, you are given a set of steps in the wrong order, and you must work out the correct order. To do this well, it helps to know that there are two common workflows behind almost every AI system: the training phase, which is how the AI is built and taught in the first place, and the operating phase — sometimes called inference — which is how the AI behaves afterwards, once real people start using it.

A simple way to remember the pattern is this: something has to go in before the AI can do anything (an input), the AI then does its work (a process), a result comes out the other side (an output), and finally, a person often reacts to that result in a way that eventually improves the system (a feedback loop). Almost every correct sequence you will be asked about follows this same underlying shape.

5.3  Design trade-offs — no perfect AI

This final section introduces one of the most important ideas in the whole book: there is no such thing as a perfect AI system. Every single design decision involves a trade-off, meaning that choosing to make one thing better usually means accepting a cost somewhere else. An AI designer might improve accuracy but reduce privacy. They might make a system faster but less transparent about how it reached its answer. Understanding and honestly naming these trade-offs — rather than pretending they do not exist — is what real system design thinking looks like in practice.

| 😐  Story: The facial recognition canteen system A school is considering setting up a facial recognition system in its canteen. Instead of using a card or typing in a PIN, a student would simply look at a camera, and the system would automatically charge their lunch account. There is a real, genuine benefit here: shorter queues, and no more lost or forgotten cards. But a responsible design process has to weigh this benefit against two serious things to think about. First, the system might make more errors for some students than others, depending on which faces were included — and which were left out — of its training data. Second, storing a student's face data raises real questions about privacy: who can access this data, how long is it kept, and what else could it be used for later? Neither of these concerns is automatically a reason to reject the system outright. But a responsible school would want both concerns properly addressed, with clear answers, before deciding to go ahead. |

Key Words and Concepts

These are the key terms and concepts for Level 2. You should be able to: (1) define each one precisely, (2) give a real-world example, and (3) distinguish it from similar but different concepts.

Filter bubble / echo chamber — When an AI recommendation system shows you content matching your past preferences, gradually limiting your exposure to different perspectives.

Algorithmic bias — When an AI system produces outcomes that systematically disadvantage certain groups, usually because training data reflected existing human inequalities.

Training data — The examples used to train an AI system. Their quality, quantity, and representativeness determine what the AI can and cannot do well.

Distribution shift — When the real-world data an AI encounters after deployment is different from the data it was trained on, causing performance to degrade.

Fluency vs accuracy — AI language models produce fluent (natural-sounding) text independently of whether that text is factually accurate. Confidence of expression is not evidence of correctness.

Privacy violation — When personal data is collected, used, or shared without a person's knowledge or meaningful consent.

Informed consent — Agreeing to data collection or use after genuinely understanding what is being collected, for what purpose, and with what consequences — not just clicking 'agree'.

Meaningful human oversight — A person with genuine authority who understands and can review specific AI decisions before they have consequences — not just a person 'somewhere in the loop'.

Error rate / false positive — The proportion of AI decisions that are wrong. A false positive is when the AI incorrectly flags something as a problem that is not (e.g. removing a post that does not break rules).

Assertion-Reason (AR) — A question format where two linked statements are given. You judge whether each is true AND whether the second correctly explains the first.

Multi-select (MS) — A question format asking you to identify ALL correct answers from a list — testing completeness of understanding, not just finding one right answer.

Design trade-off — Every AI design decision involves balancing competing goals (accuracy vs privacy, speed vs oversight, personalisation vs diversity). There is no option with no costs.

Optimisation for the wrong objective — When an AI achieves its designed goal very effectively — but that goal causes harm. The system is not broken; the objective itself is the problem.
